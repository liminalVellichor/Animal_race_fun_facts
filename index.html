    Every animal has an energy bar for sprinting.

  • ⚡

    Running flat out uses it up fast.

  • 😮‍💨

Cheetahs have a tiny energy bar, so they get tired after a few seconds. Pronghorns, horses, wolves and people can keep going for a very long time.

        For teachers and parents

        The race page keeps things simple for children. This page shows where every number comes from, how good the evidence is, and how the “getting tired” model works, so you can check it or use it in a lesson.

        Top speeds are the fastest each animal has been recorded or estimated to go, usually over a short distance. Most large-mammal figures come from driving alongside an animal and reading the speedometer, which can overestimate speed.

          The fatigue model

          Top speed lasts seconds. For longer races the page uses the critical-speed model from exercise physiology (Monod & Scherrer 1965), with a slow extra fade for efforts lasting hours, similar in spirit to Riegel’s endurance law (1981). Each racer has four numbers: a top speed, a cruise speed, a sprint reserve and a fatigue exponent.

          Average race speed by distance

          How it works

          1. Sprint. The racer runs at vmax while its sprint reserve D′ lasts. The reserve drains by however much it is running above cruise speed, so it is gone at t1 = D′ ⁄ (vmax − vc). This is the “energy bar” children see.
          2. Cruise. After that it drops to cruise speed vc, the fastest pace it can hold for several minutes.
          3. Fade. Beyond τ = 5 min, cruise speed slowly falls: v(t) = vc · (t ⁄ τ)−k. A bigger k means faster fading.
          4. Finish. Distance is the area under v(t), which has an exact formula. The finish time is the T at which d(T) = race distance, found numerically.

          For humans the four numbers were fitted to the men’s world records from 1500 m to the marathon. The model reproduces each record to within a few seconds:

          Limitations

          Only the humans, the horse and the pronghorn are fitted to real performances; the pronghorn’s long-distance fade is assumed, because data stop at 6 km. Every other animal gets values for its body type, so its long-distance results are informed guesses. The model ignores acceleration from a standing start, terrain, heat and race tactics. In the real Man v Horse race in Wales, about 35 km of hills and rivers, horses usually win but runners have won five times in 45 editions, a closer contest than this flat-road model suggests. Habitat match-ups (a falcon against a fish) are for fun only.
